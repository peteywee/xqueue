**B1 · Training time** *(the strongest post in this set)*
```
If your cooks do training modules at home on their phones,
you may be accruing wages you aren't paying.

Under the FLSA, training time is generally compensable
unless ALL FOUR of these are true:

- outside regular working hours
- genuinely voluntary
- not directly related to the job
- no productive work performed

Station training fails #3 by definition. That's the whole
point of it.

Multiply the untracked minutes by your headcount, then by
the lookback period. That's the number.
```

**B2 · Tip pools**
```
Supervisors and managers cannot receive money from a tip pool.

Not "shouldn't." Cannot.

They can keep tips a guest hands them for a table they personally
served. What they cannot do is take a share of everyone else's.

And if the person you call a shift lead has real authority over
scheduling, discipline, or hiring, a regulator may treat them as a
supervisor regardless of what the title says.

The title is not the test. The duties are.
```

**B3 · Exempt status** *(update — this changed in May 2026)*
```
The federal salary floor for overtime exemption moved backward
this year and a lot of operators missed it.

The 2024 rule that was raising it got vacated in court, and in
May 2026 the DOL formally dropped it. We are back to the 2019
levels: $684 a week, $35,568 a year.

But salary was never the whole test. Exemption needs a salary
basis AND a duties test.

A kitchen manager who mostly cooks is probably not exempt no
matter what the offer letter says or what they're paid.

Misclassification settles in back wages, and it settles for
everyone in the role — not just the person who complained.
```

**B4 · Retaliation**
```
Retaliation is the most frequently filed charge with the EEOC.
More than any single type of discrimination.

The agency took in over 91,000 charges last fiscal year and
retaliation led the list, as it has for years.

Here's what surprises managers: it's independently actionable.
The underlying complaint can be completely meritless and the
retaliation claim can still succeed.

Meaning you can win the argument and lose the case, because of
how someone got scheduled the week after they complained.
```

**B5 · Concerted activity**
```
Two cooks discussing their pay in the walk-in are engaged in
protected concerted activity under Section 7 of the NLRA.

No union required. It protects unrepresented employees too.

Policies telling staff not to discuss wages are on very old,
very shaky ground, and a lot of handbooks still contain them.
```

**B6 · FMLA threshold**
```
FMLA doesn't apply to most independent restaurants.

It requires 50+ employees within 75 miles, plus the employee
having 12 months and 1,250 hours of service.

Worth knowing in both directions: single-unit operators
often think they're covered when they aren't, and multi-unit
operators often think they aren't when they are.
```

**B7 · General Duty Clause**
```
"There's no OSHA standard for that" is not the defense people
think it is.

The General Duty Clause requires a workplace free from
recognized hazards likely to cause death or serious harm —
whether or not a specific standard exists.

A recognized hazard is one your industry knows about. Kitchens
are full of them, and "no rule covers this" doesn't help.
```

**B8 · Complaint procedure**
```
A functioning complaint process isn't HR paperwork. It's a
legal asset.

Where no tangible employment action occurred, an employer may
have a defense if it exercised reasonable care to prevent and
correct harassment and the employee unreasonably failed to use
the complaint process.

That defense requires the process to actually exist and
actually work. A policy nobody has read protects nobody.
```

**B9 · Off the clock**
```
"Clock out, then finish your side work" is the single most
expensive sentence a manager says.

Work you know about, or should know about, is compensable
whether or not it's on the timecard. Suffered or permitted is
the standard, and it does not require you to have asked.

The fifteen minutes after clock-out. The prep before clock-in.
The manager who "just finishes up."

None of that is a favor. It's unrecorded wages, and the records
are your responsibility to keep, not theirs.
```

**B10 · Rounding**
```
Rounding time is generally permitted. Rounding down is not.

The rule courts have applied is that a rounding practice has to
be neutral — over time it should average out, gaining employees
as much as it costs them.

A system that rounds to the nearest quarter hour in both
directions is usually fine. A system that rounds clock-ins
forward and clock-outs backward is a wage claim with a paper
trail attached.

Check which one your POS is actually doing. Most operators have
never looked.
```

**B11 · Regular rate**
```
Overtime isn't 1.5x the hourly rate. It's 1.5x the regular rate,
and those are different numbers more often than people think.

Nondiscretionary bonuses — attendance bonuses, production
bonuses, anything promised in advance to drive behavior — have
to be folded into the regular rate before the overtime multiplier
is applied.

Pay a $200 monthly attendance bonus and you may owe additional
overtime on every OT hour that month.

The bonus everyone thought was generous is frequently the thing
that creates the violation.
```

**B12 · The 80/20 rule is gone**
```
If you're still training managers on the 80/20 tip credit rule,
you're teaching a regulation that no longer exists.

The DOL's 80/20/30 rule was vacated by the Fifth Circuit in 2024
and the department withdrew it.

That doesn't mean side work is unlimited. The older dual jobs
concept is still there — an employee working a genuinely
separate non-tipped job is owed full minimum wage for that time.

And states set their own rules on top, some far stricter than
federal.

The federal quantitative test went away. The exposure didn't.
```

**B13 · Uniform deductions**
```
You can require the uniform. Whether you can charge for it
depends on what it does to the wage.

Under federal law, the cost of a required uniform and its upkeep
generally can't cut an employee's pay below minimum wage in that
workweek, and it can't cut into overtime pay.

Which matters most for exactly the people you'd charge — new
hires at or near the floor.

Several states go further and prohibit the deduction outright.
The line is not the same everywhere, and the deduction is
usually smaller than the claim.
```

**B14 · Breaks**
```
There is no federal law requiring you to give anyone a lunch
break. That surprises people in both directions.

What federal law says is conditional: short breaks, roughly 20
minutes or less, are generally compensable if you offer them.
Bona fide meal periods, generally 30 minutes or more, aren't —
but only if the employee is actually relieved of duty.

A cook eating at the pass while watching the window is not
relieved of duty. That's a paid meal period whether you coded it
that way or not.

And plenty of states do require breaks. Federal silence isn't
permission.
```

**B15 · Travel time**
```
Sending a cook to your other location mid-shift is paid time.
Their drive to work in the morning isn't.

Ordinary home-to-work commuting isn't compensable. But travel
between job sites during the workday generally is, and so is
travel that's part of the job's principal activity.

The run to the restaurant supply store. The shift split across
two units. The manager covering another location for four hours.

If you're moving people between buildings, that's not commuting.
That's the workday.
```

**B16 · On-call**
```
"Call in at 3 to see if we need you" may or may not be paid time,
and the test isn't what you call it.

The question is whether the employee is engaged to wait or
waiting to be engaged. If the restrictions are tight enough that
they can't effectively use the time for themselves, it's
generally compensable.

Ten-minute response radius, phone monitored, can't drink, can't
leave town: that's engaged to wait.

"We'll text you if it gets busy" usually isn't.

The more control you keep, the more likely you're buying the
time.
```

**B17 · Waiting time**
```
The dead thirty minutes before the doors open is almost always
paid.

Waiting that's controlled by the employer and short enough that
the employee can't use it for their own purposes is hours worked.
Being on premises, in uniform, waiting for a delivery or a rush
is exactly that.

The instinct is to clock people in when it gets busy.

But if you told them to be there, and they're there, the fact
that there's nothing to do yet is your problem, not theirs.
```

**B18 · Minors**
```
The rules for a 16-year-old and a 17-year-old in your kitchen are
not the same, and the equipment is where operators get caught.

Federal hazardous occupation orders restrict minors under 18 from
operating power-driven meat slicers, grinders, and certain mixers
and bakery machines — including cleaning or setting them up.

Under 16, the restrictions widen a lot: cooking is limited,
baking is generally out, and hours are capped by school schedule.

Every state layers its own rules on top, and several are
stricter.

The slicer is the one. It gets more kitchens cited than anything
else on the list.
```

**B19 · Recordkeeping**
```
When time records are missing, the tie doesn't go to the
employer.

The FLSA puts the recordkeeping obligation on you. Where an
employer's records are inadequate, courts have allowed employees
to prove hours by reasonable inference — and the burden shifts to
the employer to rebut it.

Practical translation: "we don't have records for that period"
is not a defense. It's closer to the opposite.

Three years of payroll records. Two years of the underlying time
data. Longer in some states.

The records are cheap. The inference is not.
```

**B20 · Contractors**
```
Calling your dishwasher a 1099 contractor doesn't make him one,
and the agreement he signed doesn't either.

Classification turns on the economic reality of the relationship
— control over the work, opportunity for profit or loss,
investment, permanence, skill, and how integral the work is to
your business.

A dishwasher working your hours, with your equipment, on your
schedule, doing work central to a restaurant, is an employee
under any version of that analysis.

Misclassification reaches back wages, overtime, and the payroll
taxes — plus whatever the state assesses on top.
```

**B21 · Joint employment**
```
Franchisees get sued alongside franchisors, and the question is
never what the franchise agreement says.

Joint employer analysis looks at who actually controls the terms
and conditions of employment — hiring, firing, supervision,
scheduling, pay, and the manner of work.

The standard has moved several times in the last decade and will
move again.

Which is exactly why the franchise agreement isn't the shield
operators think it is. What matters is who is really running the
schedule.
```

**B22 · At-will**
```
At-will employment means you can terminate for any reason or no
reason. It does not mean any reason.

The exceptions are the whole subject. You can't terminate for a
protected characteristic, for protected activity, for taking
protected leave, for reporting a safety hazard, for filing a
comp claim, or for protected concerted activity.

That's a long list, and it covers most of the situations where
someone is actually being fired.

"We're at-will" is a true sentence that answers almost none of
the questions people use it to answer.
```

**B23 · Coverage thresholds**
```
Half of the federal employment laws people worry about may not
apply to a single-unit restaurant.

Title VII, the ADA, and GINA generally start at 15 employees.
The ADEA at 20. FMLA at 50 within 75 miles.

But the FLSA reaches nearly every restaurant, the NLRA reaches
most private employers regardless of size, and OSHA covers you
from your first employee.

And states set their own thresholds — several apply
discrimination law at one employee.

Knowing which laws actually reach you is the first hour of work,
and almost nobody does it.
```

**B24 · Harassment standard**
```
A hostile work environment claim generally requires conduct that
is severe OR pervasive. Not both.

That "or" does a lot of work.

One incident can qualify if it's serious enough. A steady drip of
smaller conduct can qualify without any single instance being
dramatic.

Kitchens run on a culture of sharp talk, and the honest version
of this is that the pervasive branch is where the industry's
exposure actually sits.

Nobody gets there through one Saturday. They get there through
eight months of Saturdays.
```

**B25 · Religious accommodation**
```
The bar for refusing a religious scheduling request went up
sharply and most operators never got the memo.

For decades employers relied on a de minimis reading — anything
more than trivial cost was enough to refuse.

The Supreme Court rejected that in 2023. The employer now has to
show the burden is substantial in the overall context of the
business.

"It would be inconvenient to reschedule" was the old answer.
It is not the current one.
```

**B26 · Interactive process**
```
The most common ADA mistake in restaurants isn't refusing an
accommodation. It's never having the conversation.

When an employee asks for a change tied to a medical condition,
the employer is generally expected to engage in an interactive
process — an actual back-and-forth about what's needed and what's
workable.

Skipping it is itself a problem, even when the accommodation
would have been denied on the merits.

You don't have to say yes. You do have to ask.
```

**B27 · Essential functions**
```
Write your job descriptions before you need them, because after
you need them nobody believes them.

An ADA analysis asks whether the employee can perform the
essential functions of the position, with or without reasonable
accommodation. Essential means fundamental, not marginal.

Courts look at what the job actually requires — including a
written description prepared before the position was advertised.

A description written the week of a dispute carries roughly the
weight you'd expect.

Two hours, once, per position. It's the cheapest documentation in
the building.
```

**B28 · PWFA**
```
The Pregnant Workers Fairness Act is newer than most restaurant
handbooks and it changed the default.

For covered employers — 15 or more employees — it requires
reasonable accommodation for known limitations related to
pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions, absent
undue hardship.

The practical list is small and cheap: a stool, more bathroom
breaks, water on the line, a temporary lifting limit, schedule
adjustment.

It doesn't require a disability. That's the part people get
wrong — the old analysis asked whether the condition was severe
enough. This one doesn't.
```

**B29 · Age**
```
Age discrimination protection starts at 40, applies at 20
employees federally, and runs in only one direction.

The ADEA protects workers 40 and over. It does not protect
younger workers from being passed over in favor of older ones.

Where restaurants get into trouble is language. "We want
someone with energy." "Looking for a younger vibe." "He might
not keep up."

None of that has to be in a termination letter to matter. It
just has to have been said in front of someone who remembers it.
```

**B30 · Equal Pay Act**
```
The Equal Pay Act has no employee-count threshold and almost no
restaurant has ever run the analysis.

It requires equal pay for jobs requiring substantially equal
skill, effort, and responsibility under similar working
conditions.

Titles don't control. Two cooks doing substantially the same work
at different rates need a defensible reason — seniority, merit,
production, or something other than sex.

"That's what he negotiated" is not one of the listed defenses,
and it's the most common answer operators give.
```

**B31 · I-9**
```
The I-9 mistakes that get penalized are almost never about who
was hired. They're about paperwork.

Section 1 by the first day of work. Section 2 within three
business days. You cannot specify which documents an employee
presents, and asking for more than required is its own violation.

Over-documentation is a real claim. So is re-verifying a
permanent resident card that doesn't need it.

Fines are per form. A restaurant with sixty hires a year and a
sloppy binder is carrying a number most owners have never
estimated.
```

**B32 · Texas non-subscribers**
```
Texas is the one state where an employer can legally opt out of
workers' compensation, and a lot of restaurants do.

Here's the trade most operators don't fully price.

Carrying comp gets you the exclusive remedy — an injured employee
is generally limited to the comp system and can't sue you in tort.

Opt out and you lose that. A non-subscriber can be sued directly,
and several of the usual defenses are stripped away by statute.

Opting out saves premium. What it costs is the ceiling on what a
single fryer burn can become.
```

**B33 · OSHA reporting**
```
The OSHA reporting clock is shorter than most managers think and
it starts at the incident, not at the paperwork.

A work-related fatality: 8 hours.
An in-patient hospitalization, amputation, or loss of an eye: 24
hours.

That's a phone call, not a form, and "we were dealing with the
emergency" doesn't pause it.

Separately, most employers keep the 300 log and post the annual
summary. Small businesses in some categories are exempt from the
log — but nobody is exempt from the reporting deadlines.
```

**B34 · Safety retaliation**
```
An employee who refuses to work over a safety concern may be
protected even if they turn out to be wrong.

Section 11(c) protects employees who report hazards or exercise
rights under the Act. And where an employee reasonably believes
there's a real danger of death or serious harm, there's no time
to get it inspected, and they've asked the employer to fix it,
a refusal can be protected.

Reasonable belief. Not correctness.

Which means the disciplinary write-up you're drafting has a
second audience you weren't planning for.
```

**B35 · Unemployment**
```
Most terminations you'd describe as "for cause" will still result
in benefits being paid, and that surprises operators every time.

Unemployment misconduct is a narrower standard than performance
problems. Poor performance, inability to keep up, honest
mistakes, and being a bad fit generally don't disqualify.

Willful disregard of the employer's interest is closer to the
line.

Contesting every claim is a strategy that costs staff hours and
usually loses. Documenting the ones that actually meet the
standard is the version that works.
```

**B36 · Non-competes**
```
The federal non-compete ban is dead. Your state law is the whole
story now.

The FTC's rule was blocked in court, and in early 2026 the
commission formally removed it from the regulations and shifted
to case-by-case enforcement.

That is not the same as non-competes being fine. Several states
ban them outright for most workers, several more cap them by
wage, and courts everywhere scrutinize scope and duration.

For an hourly cook, a non-compete is usually unenforceable and
always a bad look.

For your GM with the vendor list, the answer is a state-law
question with a real answer. Get it.
```

**B37 · Arbitration**
```
An arbitration agreement in your onboarding packet does less than
whoever sold it to you implied.

They're broadly enforceable, and they do keep most individual
claims out of court and out of class actions.

But sexual assault and sexual harassment claims can no longer be
forced into arbitration by a pre-dispute agreement — federal law
changed that in 2022, and the employee chooses.

Arbitration also isn't cheap. In many consumer and employment
frameworks the employer carries most of the forum cost, which
changes the math on small claims considerably.

It's a real tool. It just isn't the wall it gets sold as.
```

**B38 · Final paychecks**
```
Final pay timing is a state question and the penalties are wildly
out of proportion to the amount.

Some states require payment on the last day for an involuntary
termination. Others allow the next regular payday. Several
distinguish quit from fired.

The penalty in a number of states is a continuing daily wage —
which is how a $340 final check turns into a four-figure claim
over a paperwork delay.

Look up your state's number once and put it on the termination
checklist. It's a ten-minute task that never has to be done
again.
```

**B39 · Deductions**
```
Charging a server for a walkout is one of the most common wage
violations in the industry.

Under federal law, deductions for cash shortages, breakage,
walkouts, and till discrepancies generally can't take an
employee below minimum wage for the workweek, and can't cut into
overtime.

Many states prohibit them entirely regardless of the wage —
treating them as the employer's cost of doing business.

It feels fair. It's frequently illegal, and it is exceptionally
easy to prove, because you wrote it on the paperwork yourself.
```

**B40 · Background checks**
```
If you run background checks through a third party, the FCRA
applies to you, and it has a sequence.

Standalone written disclosure — its own document, not buried in
the application. Written authorization. Then, before you act on
anything adverse, a pre-adverse action notice with a copy of the
report and a summary of rights, a reasonable window to respond,
and only then the final notice.

The most common failure is the first step: putting the
disclosure inside the job application. That alone has driven
class actions.

The process is free. Skipping it isn't.
```

**B41 · Handbooks**
```
A handbook can create the contract you wrote it to avoid.

Language that promises progressive discipline, guarantees process
before termination, or lists the only reasons someone can be let
go has been read by courts as contractual in a number of states.

The fix is boring: a clear at-will statement, an acknowledgment
signed at hire, discretionary language throughout, and a stated
right to modify.

Then actually follow what's left. A handbook you don't follow is
worse than the one you don't have, because now the deviation is
documented.
```

**B42 · Documentation**
```
The write-up you didn't do is the one the other side gets to
describe.

Contemporaneous documentation — dated, specific, factual, shared
with the employee — is the single highest-return administrative
habit in a restaurant.

Not because it wins cases on its own. Because its absence lets an
eight-month performance history get retold as a two-week
retaliation story, and nothing in your files contradicts it.

Specific beats harsh. "Third late arrival this month, 22 minutes,
discussed 3/14" does more than a paragraph about attitude.
```

**B43 · Work rules**
```
The standard for whether your handbook rules are lawful under
federal labor law has flipped twice in eight years and may flip
again.

The current framing asks whether a rule would tend to chill a
reasonable employee from exercising Section 7 rights — and the
employer bears the burden of justifying it.

That reaches ordinary policies: confidentiality, media contact,
civility, recording bans.

The board's composition drives this standard, and it changes with
administrations.

Practical read: write rules narrowly and say why they exist. A
rule with a stated business justification survives more versions
of this test than a broad one does.
```

**B44 · Predictive scheduling**
```
In a growing number of cities and states, changing the schedule
after you post it costs money.

Fair workweek laws typically require advance notice — often 14
days — and require predictability pay when you change, cancel, or
add a shift inside the window. Some require rest between closing
and opening shifts, and offering hours to existing staff before
hiring.

They're local. Most of the country has nothing. A handful of
jurisdictions have all of it.

If you operate in one and don't know it, your standard practice
of texting the Friday change on Wednesday is a per-shift penalty.
```

**B45 · Recording**
```
Whether an employee can legally record a conversation with you
depends on which state you're standing in.

Most states allow recording with one party's consent — the person
recording counts. A minority require all parties to consent.

Two things follow.

Your no-recording policy has federal labor law problems in some
applications, so it isn't the clean fix it looks like.

And in a one-party state, the disciplinary conversation you're
about to have may already be evidence.

Manage as though it's recorded. In a lot of places, it is.
```
