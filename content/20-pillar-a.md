### Pillar A — Translation

*A business concept, shown operating inside a kitchen. This is the moat. 72 posts.*

**A1 · Operating leverage** *(pinned post; attach figure 14)*
```
Restaurants don't die in a bad year. They die in a slow February.

Your rent, insurance, salaried managers, and equipment notes don't
care how many covers you did. Those costs are fixed.

That means every cover above break-even is almost pure profit,
and every cover below it is almost pure loss.

There's a name for this. It's called operating leverage, and it's
the same structure that makes airlines and software companies
behave the way they do.

Nobody tells kitchen people this. It explains almost everything.
```

**A2 · Contribution margin**
```
The $28 steak is not your best-selling item. The $3 soda is.

Steak: $28 price, maybe $9 food cost. $19 contribution.
Soda: $3 price, maybe $0.30 cost. $2.70 contribution.

Now count how many of each you sell in a night.

Contribution margin per item x volume is the number that
matters. Menu price alone tells you almost nothing.
```

**A3 · Sunk cost**
```
The walk-in is full of product you over-ordered.

That money is gone. It is gone whether you run the special
or not. It cannot be recovered by any decision you make today.

So the only real question is: does running this special make
more money than running something else tonight?

If the answer is no, the full walk-in is not a reason. That's
a sunk cost, and it's the most expensive kind of thinking in
any kitchen.
```

**A4 · Expectancy theory** *(attach figure 9)*
```
Your best cook stopped caring. Here's the actual diagnosis.

Motivation is three things multiplied together:

Can my effort actually produce good performance?
Will good performance actually get recognized?
Do I want what recognition gets me?

Multiplied. Not added.

A zero anywhere makes the whole thing zero. Which is why the
cook who's been passed over twice works at exactly the speed
required and not one step faster. He didn't get lazy. One of
those three went to zero and nobody checked which.
```

**A5 · Variance analysis**
```
Your food cost came in under budget. That might be bad news.

Cost variance splits into two parts: price and quantity.

You paid less per case — favorable price variance. But if you
bought lower-grade produce, you'll see it in waste, in prep
time, and in plates coming back.

"Favorable" means different from standard. It does not mean good.
Chasing favorable price variance is how kitchens quietly wreck
their own food.
```

**A6 · The constrained resource**
```
Your menu is not limited by ingredients. It's limited by the
fryer.

When one station is the bottleneck, the item to push isn't the
one with the highest margin. It's the one with the highest
margin per minute of the bottleneck.

A $19 plate that ties up the fryer for four minutes loses to a
$14 plate that takes ninety seconds. Every time. All night.

Margin per plate is the wrong denominator when something is
constrained.
```

**A7 · Cash conversion cycle**
```
An underrated fact about restaurants: the cash cycle is
almost free money.

Most businesses buy inventory, wait 60 days to sell it, then
wait another 45 to get paid. That gap has to be financed.

You buy on Tuesday, sell on Friday, get paid instantly, and
pay your vendor in 30 days.

You're operating on your suppliers' money. That's a real
structural advantage and almost nobody in the industry says
it out loud.
```

**A8 · Opportunity cost**
```
The owner working the line for free is the most expensive
labor in the building.

Not because of what it costs. Because of what it prevents.

Those are the hours that would have gone to vendor
negotiation, menu engineering, hiring, or getting the second
location open. None of that shows up on a P&L, which is
exactly why it never gets counted.

Free labor is only free if the alternative was doing nothing.
```

**A9 · Break-even in covers**
```
Stop calculating break-even in dollars. Calculate it in covers.

Fixed costs divided by contribution margin per cover gives you
a number your whole team can actually use.

"We need $4,180 tonight" means nothing to a line.
"We need 143 covers before we're making money" means
something to everyone.

Same math. One of them changes behavior.
```

**A10 · Tuckman**
```
New crew fighting in week three isn't a hiring mistake.

Teams go through forming, storming, norming, performing.
Storming is a stage, not a malfunction.

The crew that never argues in week three usually isn't
harmonious. The conflict just went somewhere you can't see,
and it comes back at the worst possible service.
```

**A11 · Accrual vs cash**
```
The month you felt rich is usually the month you lost money.

Cash accounting says you made money when money hit the account.
Accrual says you made money when you actually earned it.

A big banquet deposit in March for an April event is April's
revenue sitting in March's bank balance. Spend it in March and
you've spent income you haven't earned yet.

Every operator who "doesn't know where it went" is running the
business on the bank balance instead of the books.
```

**A12 · Depreciation**
```
Your combi oven costs you money every single month, including
the months you don't pay for it.

You wrote one check. But that oven has maybe eight years in it,
and each year burns through an eighth of what you paid.

Depreciation isn't an accounting trick. It's the honest version
of what equipment costs.

The operator who thinks the oven is "paid off" is the one with
no money set aside when it dies mid-service in year nine.
```

**A13 · Working capital**
```
Profitable restaurants close all the time. Here's the mechanism.

Working capital is what you have on hand minus what's coming due
in the next stretch. Inventory and receivables on one side,
vendor bills and payroll on the other.

Profit is an opinion at month end. Payroll is a fact on Friday.

You can be up on paper for the quarter and still not make Friday.
That gap is where most closures actually happen.
```

**A14 · Inventory turnover**
```
Every case sitting in your dry storage is money you already spent
that isn't earning anything.

Turnover is how many times you sell through and replace your
inventory in a period. High turns mean your money keeps cycling.
Low turns mean it's sitting on a shelf going stale.

The temptation is to buy deep on a deal. Sometimes right.

But a case discount you'll take four months to sell through is
usually a worse deal than the price on the invoice suggests.
```

**A15 · Standard cost**
```
A recipe card is a standard cost, and most kitchens treat it as
a suggestion.

The whole point of writing down eight ounces is so that when
food cost moves, you know whether it was price or portion.

Without the standard, you have one number going the wrong
direction and no way to find out why.

Nobody costs a recipe because they enjoy costing recipes. They
do it so next month's problem is diagnosable.
```

**A16 · Overhead allocation**
```
Your catering arm might be losing money and your P&L would
never show it.

Overhead — rent, utilities, salaried managers — has to get
assigned somewhere. Most operations spread it by revenue share
because that's easy.

But if catering uses half the walk-in and a third of the prep
labor while making a fifth of the revenue, allocating by revenue
makes it look profitable and makes the dining room look worse
than it is.

How you split the shared costs decides which part of the business
you think is working.
```

**A17 · Prime cost**
```
Food cost is the number everyone watches and it's the wrong one
to watch alone.

Prime cost is food plus labor together. Around 60% to 65% of
sales is the range most full-service operations aim for, and the
two halves trade against each other constantly.

Buy pre-cut produce: food cost up, prep labor down.
Butcher in house: food cost down, labor up.

Manage them separately and you'll win one and lose the other
without noticing you broke even.
```

**A18 · Relevant range**
```
"Fixed cost" has an expiration date and nobody mentions it.

Your rent is fixed — until you add covers you can't seat and
need a second location. Your salaried manager is fixed — until
volume needs two of them.

Costs are only fixed inside a relevant range of activity. Push
past it and the fixed cost steps up all at once.

Growth doesn't hurt because of the food. It hurts because of the
step you didn't see coming.
```

**A19 · Step costs**
```
The 41st cover an hour can cost you $600 a week.

Not because of food. Because it's the cover that means you need
another server on the floor, and that person costs the same
whether they run four tables or one.

Labor doesn't scale smoothly. It scales in bodies.

Which is why the schedule is a series of cliffs, not a slope, and
why "just add a little more volume" isn't always the win it looks
like on paper.
```

**A20 · Profit vs cash flow**
```
Profit is what you earned. Cash is what you can spend. They are
not the same number and they don't arrive at the same time.

You booked the wedding — that's profit in the month you serve it.
You bought the product — that's cash out in the week you ordered.
You paid the crew — cash out on Friday.

Every business that fails while profitable fails in that gap.

Watch both. If you only get to watch one, watch the cash.
```

**A21 · Time value of money**
```
A dollar this Friday genuinely beats a dollar next quarter, and
not because of inflation.

That dollar can buy product that becomes four dollars of covers
this weekend. Money has a job it could be doing, and time it
spends not doing that job is a real cost.

This is why a vendor offering 2% off for paying in ten days
instead of thirty is a much bigger deal than 2% sounds.

Run the annualized number sometime. It's not close.
```

**A22 · Net present value**
```
"It pays for itself in two years" is not an analysis.

NPV asks a harder question: if I take every dollar this thing
will earn, discount each one for how long I have to wait, and
subtract what it costs today — is what's left positive?

The discount is the point. A dollar in year four is worth less
than a dollar in year one, so a project that's back-loaded is
worth less than the same total spread evenly.

Two projects can return identical totals and only one is worth
doing.
```

**A23 · Payback period**
```
Payback period is the easiest capital rule to use and the easiest
one to get burned by.

It tells you how long until you're whole. It tells you nothing
about what happens after.

A $12,000 machine that pays back in 18 months and dies at month
20 loses to one that pays back in 30 months and runs a decade.

Use payback to screen. Never use it to decide.
```

**A24 · Cost of capital**
```
There is no such thing as free money in your business, including
your own.

If you put $40,000 of your own savings into the buildout, that
money had somewhere else to be. Whatever it would have safely
earned is now the minimum this restaurant has to clear before
you're actually ahead.

That's your cost of capital, and it applies to owner money
exactly like it applies to the bank's.

The bank sends a statement. Your own money doesn't, which is why
it's the one people forget to count.
```

**A25 · Financial leverage**
```
Debt makes a good year better and a bad year fatal. That's the
whole trade.

Borrow to open the second location and you keep all the upside
above the payment. That's leverage working.

But the payment is fixed, and it doesn't move when a highway
project closes your access road for five months.

Operating leverage already made your cost structure rigid.
Financial leverage stacks a second rigid layer on top of it.

Two rigid structures and one soft season is the standard story.
```

**A26 · Price elasticity**
```
You raised prices 6% and covers fell 3%. You made money.

Elasticity is how much demand moves when price moves. If demand
moves less than price did, the increase wins even though you're
serving fewer people.

Most operators only see the covers going down and reverse it.

Do the arithmetic before you panic. Fewer people paying more is
frequently the better night, and it's an easier night to staff.
```

**A27 · Marginal thinking**
```
The right question is almost never "is this profitable." It's
"is the next one profitable."

Averages tell you about the past. Margins tell you about the
decision in front of you.

Should you stay open the extra hour? Total profitability of the
restaurant is irrelevant. What matters is whether the covers in
that hour beat the labor, utilities, and wear in that hour.

Businesses that think in averages keep doing profitable things
past the point where they stopped paying.
```

**A28 · Comparative advantage**
```
Your best cook should probably not be doing the thing he's best
at.

If he's faster than everyone at both grill and prep, the question
isn't what he's best at. It's where the gap between him and the
next person is biggest.

Put him where his advantage is largest, not where his skill is
highest. Give away the rest even if he'd do it better.

This is comparative advantage, it's two hundred years old, and it
is the single most useful idea for writing a schedule.
```

**A29 · Diminishing returns**
```
The fourth cook on the line makes less difference than the third.
The sixth might make things worse.

Add labor to a fixed amount of equipment and space, and each
additional body produces less than the one before it. Past a
point, they start costing each other steps.

This isn't a motivation problem. It's the shape of the room.

Most "we need more people" conversations are actually "we need
more line" conversations, and the two have very different price
tags.
```

**A30 · Substitutes and complements**
```
Your competition isn't the restaurant across the street. It's the
grocery store, the meal kit, and the couch.

Substitutes are anything that satisfies the same need. When their
price or convenience improves, your demand drops, and you never
see the transaction that didn't happen.

Complements work the other way. The movie theater that closed is
why your Tuesdays got quiet, and nobody in your building would
guess it.

Trace the demand, not the block.
```

**A31 · Overjustification**
```
Paying for something people already did for free can make them
stop doing it.

You had a crew that stayed late to help each other close. You
introduced a $15 bonus for it. Within a month, nobody stays late
without asking about the bonus.

Extrinsic reward can crowd out the internal reason. The behavior
gets re-filed from "who we are" to "what it pays."

The bonus was cheap. What it replaced was not.
```

**A32 · Herzberg**
```
Fixing what people complain about will not make them happy. It
will make them stop complaining.

Two different sets of factors. Hygiene factors — pay, schedule,
conditions, the broken AC — cause dissatisfaction when they're
bad. Fixing them gets you to neutral.

Motivators — responsibility, growth, recognition, real work —
are what actually produce satisfaction.

Fix the AC. You have to. Just don't expect gratitude, because
working AC was never a gift.
```

**A33 · Job characteristics**
```
Most kitchen jobs are demotivating by design, and none of it was
deliberate.

Five things make work feel meaningful: variety of skill, doing a
whole task instead of a fragment, work that visibly matters,
autonomy over how it's done, and feedback from the work itself.

Now picture the person who portions the same protein for six
hours and never sees a plate leave.

You didn't hire someone with a bad attitude. You built a station
with four of five missing.
```

**A34 · Social loafing**
```
Effort per person drops as the group gets bigger, and the drop is
almost never conscious.

When contribution can't be identified individually, output falls.
It's one of the oldest findings in the field and it shows up on
every closing crew that "cleaned together."

The fix isn't a speech. It's identifiability.

Assign the walk-in to a person, not to the shift. Same crew, same
hours, different result, and nobody had to be told to care more.
```

**A35 · Psychological safety**
```
The strongest predictor of whether your kitchen learns from
mistakes is whether people can admit them without cost.

Psychological safety isn't comfort or niceness. It's the shared
belief that saying "I dropped it" or "I don't know this station"
won't be used against you.

Low-safety kitchens don't have fewer errors. They have fewer
reported errors, which is much more expensive.

You'll know which one you run by what people tell you at 9pm on
a bad Saturday.
```

**A36 · Attribution error**
```
When the new guy is slow, you think he's slow. When you're slow,
you think the ticket printer jammed.

Fundamental attribution error: we explain other people with
character and ourselves with circumstance.

It matters because you staff and fire on those explanations.

Before you conclude someone is lazy, check whether they've been
handed a station with a broken low-boy and no par sheet. It costs
you ten seconds and it's right often enough to change decisions.
```

**A37 · Span of control**
```
There's a hard limit on how many people one person can actually
supervise, and it's lower than your schedule assumes.

The number moves with how complex and how variable the work is.
Simple, repetitive, stable work — one person can oversee a lot.
Fast, interdependent, constantly interrupted work — far fewer.

A Saturday dinner rush is the second kind.

If your one manager is nominally over twenty-two people during
peak, they aren't supervising. They're reacting, and the
schedule is what decided that.
```

**A38 · Groupthink**
```
The meeting where everyone agreed is the meeting that should
worry you.

Groupthink happens when a cohesive team values agreement over
accuracy. Doubts stay unspoken, alternatives never get raised,
and the group leaves more confident than the evidence supports.

Restaurant leadership teams are unusually prone to it. Small,
tight, under time pressure, loyal.

Ask the newest person first. They haven't learned yet what
they're supposed to say.
```

**A39 · Equity theory**
```
People don't evaluate their pay. They evaluate their ratio
against someone else's.

What I put in over what I get out, compared to what she puts in
over what she gets out. When those ratios don't match, people
restore balance — usually by lowering their input, because that's
the lever they control.

Which means a raise for one person can reduce output from three.

Not because they're petty. Because that's how the comparison
works, and it happens whether or not anyone talks about it.
```

**A40 · Situational leadership**
```
The same management style that saved you with one cook will lose
you another, and neither outcome was about you.

Match the approach to where the person actually is. New and
willing needs direction. Competent but shaky needs coaching.
Capable and unsure needs support. Expert and motivated needs to
be left alone.

The most common failure is running one setting on everybody.

Usually the setting that worked on the person you were.
```

**A41 · Value chain**
```
Break your restaurant into the steps that actually add value and
you'll find two or three that don't.

Receiving, storage, prep, cook, plate, serve, clear, clean. Each
one either makes the plate worth more to a guest or it doesn't.

Then there's everything supporting it — scheduling, ordering,
training, repair — which adds no value directly and consumes
plenty.

You're not looking for waste. You're looking for the step where
your version is genuinely better than the place down the street.

If you can't name it, that's the finding.
```

**A42 · Stuck in the middle**
```
The most dangerous position in any market is the middle, and it's
where most independents land by accident.

Two ways to win. Be the cheapest, which requires real cost
advantage — scale, volume, systems. Or be different enough that
price isn't the comparison.

The restaurant that's a bit nicer than fast casual and a bit
cheaper than the steakhouse competes with everyone and is chosen
by no one.

Nobody decides to be stuck in the middle. You arrive there by
splitting the difference on every decision for four years.
```

**A43 · Switching costs**
```
Your regulars aren't loyal. They're parked.

Switching cost is everything it takes a customer to go somewhere
else — learning a new menu, a new drive, the risk that it's
worse, losing the server who knows their order.

You raise switching costs with familiarity, relationships, and
knowing the regular's kid's name. That's real economics, not
hospitality theater.

Every new place that opens nearby lowers those costs for
somebody. Which is why you find out who was actually loyal in the
month after a competitor opens.
```

**A44 · Scale vs scope**
```
Two different kinds of "bigger" and operators mix them up
constantly.

Economies of scale: same thing, more volume, lower unit cost.
Buying protein for four locations instead of one.

Economies of scope: different things, shared resources. Using the
same kitchen and crew to run lunch, dinner, and catering.

Scale needs volume. Scope needs flexibility. The systems that
deliver one often make the other harder.

Adding a catering arm to chase scope while trying to standardize
for scale is how you get both cost structures and neither
advantage.
```

**A45 · Core competency**
```
A core competency has to pass three tests, and "we make good
food" passes none of them.

It has to open access to more than one market. It has to matter
visibly to the customer. And it has to be hard for a competitor
to copy.

Good food fails the third. Anyone can hire your cook.

A hiring and training system that reliably turns inexperienced
people into competent line cooks in three weeks passes all three.
That one is worth building. The recipe isn't.
```

**A46 · Value innovation**
```
The interesting move isn't beating your competitors. It's not
being compared to them.

Take the standard package in your category and ask which parts
you can cut hard, which you can raise hard, and what nobody in
your segment offers at all.

Cut table service, raise ingredient quality, add speed — that's
a whole category, and it wasn't invented by winning the old
comparison.

Everyone competing on the same handful of variables is a signal.
The variables are the thing to question.
```

**A47 · Make or buy**
```
"We make it in house" is a strategy decision that mostly gets
made by pride.

Make it yourself when it's genuinely better, when it's what
you're known for, or when doing it yourself teaches you
something you need.

Buy it when a supplier does it cheaper at the same quality and
the customer can't tell.

House-made bread when it's the thing people mention: correct.
House-made bread because buying it feels like giving up: that's
forty labor hours a week bought with feelings.
```

**A48 · First mover disadvantage**
```
Being first to a concept in your market is often a bad hand.

You pay for the education. You explain what the thing is, train
customers to want it, make the expensive mistakes, and discover
which parts don't work.

The second entrant reads all of that for free and opens a
cleaner version across the street.

First mover advantage is real, but it needs something to lock it
in — a location, a supply relationship, a brand people already
attached to. Without one of those, being early is just paying
tuition for the competition.
```

**A49 · Segmentation**
```
"Everyone" is not a market and it's the most expensive answer to
give.

Segment on behavior, not demographics. The useful groups are
things like weekday lunch under 45 minutes, Friday date night,
and the family that needs to be out before bedtime.

Same person can be in all three on different days. The demo tells
you nothing. The occasion tells you everything.

Menu, pacing, staffing, and pricing should be answering one of
those. Trying to answer all three at once is what a confused
dining room feels like from the inside.
```

**A50 · Anchoring**
```
Put one expensive item on the menu that almost nobody orders. It
earns its space anyway.

The $58 tomahawk isn't there to sell. It's there so the $34 ribeye
reads as reasonable.

People don't evaluate prices in isolation. They evaluate them
against whatever else is in view, and you control what's in view.

This is real and it's well documented. It also stops working the
moment the anchor looks absurd, which is where menu design turns
back into judgment.
```

**A51 · Product life cycle**
```
Every menu item is somewhere on a curve, and the mistake is
managing them all the same way.

Introduction: nobody orders it, needs a push.
Growth: sales climbing, protect the supply.
Maturity: steady, this is where your money is made.
Decline: slipping, and it's taking up a station.

The item people loved four years ago that now sells nine a week
isn't a tradition. It's a station slot, a prep task, and three
SKUs in the walk-in.

Menus don't get bloated by adding. They get bloated by not
subtracting.
```

**A52 · Lifetime value**
```
The regular who spends $31 a visit isn't a $31 customer. She's a
$4,800 customer, and you've been treating her like the first
number.

Lifetime value is what a relationship is worth over its whole
run — spend per visit, times visits per year, times years,
adjusted for margin.

Once you've done that arithmetic, comping a $14 entree on a bad
night stops looking like a loss and starts looking like the
cheapest retention spending available.

The comp isn't generosity. It's math.
```

**A53 · Loss leader**
```
Selling something at a loss on purpose is a legitimate strategy
and a very easy one to run badly.

It works when the losing item reliably pulls a basket that pays
for it. Cheap wings that come with a table of drinks.

It fails when people take the loss item and leave, or when it
cannibalizes something profitable they'd have ordered anyway.

The test isn't whether the promotion moved. It's whether the
average check on promotion nights beats the average check on
normal ones. That's a different number and most operators never
pull it.
```

**A54 · Brand equity**
```
Brand isn't your logo. It's the price premium you can charge for
a plate the guest can't otherwise distinguish.

If you can sell the same burger for four dollars more than the
place with the same burger, that four dollars is brand equity and
it's a real asset on a balance sheet you don't keep.

It's built slowly through consistency and destroyed quickly
through variance.

Which means the shift where you ran out of the thing you're known
for cost you more than that shift's tickets.
```

**A55 · Sample size**
```
Three bad reviews in a week is not a trend. It might not even be
information.

Small samples swing wildly for no reason at all. Flip a fair coin
five times and you'll get four heads often enough that you'd
never notice.

The bigger the sample, the closer it sits to the truth. Small
samples produce the extremes in both directions — which is also
why one perfect week doesn't prove the fix worked.

Before you rip up the prep system over a rough Thursday, ask how
many Thursdays you're actually looking at.
```

**A56 · Correlation**
```
Sales are up on the nights your best server works. That does not
mean she's causing it.

She might be scheduled on the busy nights. She might be scheduled
on the nights the manager who runs a tight floor is on. She might
be causing it.

Three explanations, one pattern, and the data can't tell them
apart.

The reason this matters isn't intellectual. It's that you're
about to build a schedule, or a bonus, on whichever explanation
you assumed.
```

**A57 · Regression to the mean**
```
You chewed out the crew after the worst Saturday of the quarter
and next Saturday was better. The chewing out is not why.

Extreme results are extreme partly because of luck, and luck
doesn't repeat. After an unusually bad night the next one is
better on average no matter what you do.

Which quietly trains managers that criticism works and praise
doesn't. Praise the best night, next one's worse. Criticize the
worst night, next one's better.

Both are the same statistical artifact, and one of them is
shaping how you talk to people.
```

**A58 · Averages**
```
Your average ticket time is 14 minutes and your problem is
entirely in the tail.

The average tells you nothing about the 8% of tickets that took
40 minutes — which is where every complaint, comp, and bad review
lives.

Guests don't experience your mean. They experience their ticket.

Stop reporting averages. Report the 90th percentile, and manage
that. The average will follow, and the complaints stop first.
```

**A59 · Critical path**
```
Not every prep task is equally urgent, and the schedule you're
running probably treats them as if they are.

The critical path is the longest chain of tasks that must happen
in sequence. Anything on it delays open, one minute for one
minute. Anything off it has slack.

Braising is on the path. Rolling silverware isn't.

Which means the right question at 9am isn't "what's left." It's
"what's on the path," and the answer changes what you hand to the
person who just walked in.
```

**A60 · Crashing vs fast-tracking**
```
Two ways to speed something up and they fail differently.

Crashing: add resources. More people on prep. Costs money,
hits diminishing returns fast, and past a point new bodies slow
the ones already there.

Fast-tracking: run things in parallel that were supposed to be
sequential. Costs no money, adds rework risk — you start plating
before the sauce is checked, and sometimes it isn't right.

Crashing buys time with money. Fast-tracking buys time with risk.

Every rushed open uses one of them. Worth knowing which you just
chose.
```

**A61 · Scope creep**
```
Nobody ever approved the menu getting to 74 items. It got there
one reasonable request at a time.

Scope creep is uncontrolled expansion without corresponding
adjustments to time, cost, or people. Each addition is small,
defensible, and requested by someone who matters.

The cost never shows up on the item. It shows up in prep hours,
walk-in space, training time, and ticket times four months later.

The discipline isn't refusing additions. It's requiring that
something comes off when something goes on.
```

**A62 · Earned value**
```
"We're on schedule and on budget" can both be true while a
project is failing.

Earned value asks a third question: how much of the work is
actually done? Compare what you planned to spend by now, what
you've actually spent, and what you've genuinely completed.

Halfway through the remodel budget with a third of the work
finished is not "on budget." It's a 33% overrun that hasn't
been billed yet.

Spend tells you about the past. Completion tells you where you'll
land.
```

**A63 · Three-point estimating**
```
Ask a cook how long the prep takes and you'll get one number.
Ask for three and you'll get the truth.

Best case, most likely, worst case. Weight the middle heavily,
average them, and you get an estimate that includes the reality
that things go wrong.

Single-point estimates are always optimistic. Not because people
lie, but because they picture the run where nothing breaks.

Three numbers takes ten extra seconds and it's the difference
between a schedule and a wish.
```

**A64 · Student syndrome**
```
Give a prep list four hours of slack and it will take four hours
of slack.

Work expands to fill the time available, and people start at the
last responsible moment. Both effects are well documented and
both are running in your kitchen right now.

Which is why padding every task is self-defeating. The buffer
gets consumed before the risk it was protecting against arrives.

Pool the buffer at the end instead of hiding it in each task.
Same total time, and it's actually there when something goes
wrong.
```

**A65 · Risk register**
```
Every operator carries a list of things that could go wrong. Almost
none of them write it down, which is why the list resets every
time a manager leaves.

A register is four columns: what could happen, how likely, how bad,
and what we'd do.

Walk-in compressor. Key opener quits. Health inspection during a
remodel. Delivery truck doesn't show Friday.

None of that is new information to you. Writing it down is what
turns it from your intuition into the operation's property.
```

**A66 · Risk appetite**
```
Appetite is how much risk you're willing to take on purpose.
Tolerance is how much you can absorb before something breaks.

Confusing them is expensive in both directions.

An operator with a big appetite and thin tolerance opens the
second location on a thin margin and loses both.

An operator with a big tolerance and no appetite sits on cash for
nine years while the lease appreciates around them.

They're different questions. Answer them separately and in that
order.
```

**A67 · Secondary risk**
```
Your response to a risk creates a new risk, and the new one is
almost never on the list.

Worried about food safety, so you add three checks per shift.
Now closing runs long, the crew rushes the last one, and the
check that mattered gets signed without being done.

Worried about turnover, so you raise starting wage. Now your
two-year people are making new-hire money and you have a
different retention problem.

The mitigation isn't wrong. It's just not free, and pretending
it is is how a control register turns into fiction.
```

**A68 · Bullwhip effect**
```
Small swings in your dining room become huge swings at the farm,
and each link amplifies the one before it.

You see a busy weekend and order 20% extra. Your distributor sees
that and orders 35% extra. The processor sees that and books 50%.

Then the weather turns, your covers normalize, and everyone up
the chain is holding product nobody ordered.

Then the correction comes back down as a shortage.

The pattern is called the bullwhip effect and it's a large part of
why your prices move for reasons nothing to do with you.
```

**A69 · JIT vs buffer**
```
Just-in-time only works when your suppliers are reliable, and it
is the first thing to break when they aren't.

Thin inventory frees cash, cuts waste, and needs less space.
It also means one missed truck is an 86 on your top seller.

Buffer stock costs cash and space and buys you the ability to
absorb someone else's failure.

The right answer changes per item, not per restaurant. Buffer the
things you're known for and the things with one supplier. Run
thin on everything with three sources and a short shelf life.
```

**A70 · Total cost of ownership**
```
The cheaper dish machine is frequently the more expensive one and
the invoice will never tell you.

Total cost of ownership is purchase price plus install, plus
chemicals, plus water and energy, plus service contracts, plus
downtime, plus what it costs to train people on it — across its
whole life.

A $3,000 difference at purchase disappears in eighteen months of
higher chemical draw.

Buyers compare the price because the price is the number that's
easy to find. That's the whole reason the cheap one keeps
winning.
```

**A71 · Single sourcing**
```
One supplier gets you a better price and hands them your
schedule.

Single sourcing concentrates volume, which buys leverage, better
terms, and a relationship where somebody actually answers the
phone on a Saturday.

It also means their strike, their recall, and their bad quarter
are now yours.

Dual sourcing costs a few points and buys optionality.

The rule most operators land on eventually: single-source the
commodities, dual-source anything the restaurant is known for.
```

**A72 · Agency problem**
```
Your GM's incentives and your incentives are not the same, and no
amount of trust changes that.

The agency problem is what happens when someone makes decisions on
your behalf while facing different consequences than you do.

Bonus on food cost and you'll get food cost — possibly by buying
down quality, cutting portions, and pushing prep labor into
someone else's line item.

They're not betraying you. They're responding to the measure you
chose, which is what measures are for.

Whatever you incentivize, you'll get. Including the version of it
you didn't want.
```
