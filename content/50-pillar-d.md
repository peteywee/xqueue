### Pillar D — Method

*27 posts. How learning and thinking actually work. Cheap to write, broadly shareable, reaches past the restaurant audience — which is where the consulting and handbook work comes from.*

**D1 · Retrieval** *(attach figure 1)*
```
Rereading your notes is close to worthless and it feels great.

That's the trap. Rereading creates fluency — the material
looks familiar, so you conclude you know it.

Fluency is not memory. Recognition is not recall.

Closing the book and trying to retrieve it feels worse and
works dramatically better. Every study technique that feels
uncomfortable is the one doing the work.
```

**D2 · Bloom's**
```
If an assignment says "evaluate" and you summarized, you
capped your own grade before you started.

Remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, create.

Upper-level rubrics are written for the top three. Explaining
what a framework is scores as description no matter how
accurate it is.

The tell: description spends its words on the framework and
mentions the company at the end. Analysis spends its words on
the company.
```

**D3 · Sealed pieces**
```
Just finished a business degree and the strangest part is how
it arrives.

Macro, then five weeks later organizational behavior, then
accounting. Each course sealed off from the others.

The connections are real. Comparative advantage in economics
is the same idea as a make-or-buy decision in strategy. But
the person teaching macro isn't the person teaching strategy,
so nobody says it.

By the capstone it's all in twenty separate boxes and you're
asked to use it at once.
```

**D4 · Frameworks as instruments**
```
Every business framework is a question to ask, not a table to
fill in.

VRIO isn't a grid you reproduce. It's four questions about one
specific resource, and the first "no" is your answer — you stop
there.

Five Forces isn't five paragraphs. It's an answer to which
single force is squeezing your margin, because that's the one
strategy has to address.

Filling in the table is how you get a good grade. Asking the
question is how you get an answer.
```

**D5 · Recurring concepts**
```
Ten ideas showed up in three or more of my courses wearing
different vocabulary each time.

Opportunity cost. Comparative advantage. The agency problem.
Fixed vs variable cost behavior. Correlation isn't causation.
Structure follows strategy.

Learning those six properly once is worth more than learning
twenty things partially.

Everything else in a business degree is application.
```

**D6 · Spacing**
```
The same four hours of study produce wildly different results
depending only on how you spread them.

Four hours the night before: you'll pass and lose most of it.
One hour a week for four weeks: you'll keep it for years.

Same time, same material, same person. The only variable is the
gaps, and the gaps are doing the work.

Forgetting a little between sessions is not a problem to solve.
It's the mechanism. Each time you claw it back, it's stored
harder.
```

**D7 · Interleaving**
```
Practicing one thing until you've got it is the most satisfying
way to study and one of the weakest.

Blocked practice — twenty of the same problem type — produces
fast, confident performance in the session and poor performance
later.

Mixing types up feels clumsy and slow. You keep having to work
out which approach applies, which is exactly the skill that
transfers, because real problems don't arrive labeled.

Performance during practice and learning from practice are
different things, and they frequently point opposite directions.
```

**D8 · Desirable difficulties**
```
There's a category of study technique that reliably makes you
worse in the moment and better later.

Spacing. Interleaving. Testing yourself before you feel ready.
Making yourself generate an answer instead of recognizing one.

All of them slow the session down. All of them raise the error
rate while you're doing them. All of them beat the smooth
version on any test given a week later.

Which is a problem, because the smooth version feels like it's
working and the effective version feels like it isn't.

Your sense of how well you're learning is the least reliable
instrument in the room.
```

**D9 · Illusion of competence**
```
Highlighting a textbook produces a beautifully marked book and
almost no memory.

The mechanism is that recognition is easy and feels like
knowing. You see the yellow line, the sentence looks familiar,
your brain returns "yes, I know this."

You don't know it. You recognize it. Those are separate
capacities and only one of them shows up on an exam or on a
shift.

Test: close the book and write the argument from memory. The gap
between how well you thought that would go and how it actually
goes is the illusion, measured.
```

**D10 · Generation**
```
Guessing before you're taught the answer makes the answer stick
harder, even when the guess is wrong.

Attempting to produce something — a definition, a solution, an
explanation — before you've been given it improves retention
compared to just being told.

The wrong guess doesn't hurt. It appears to help, as long as the
correction comes.

Which means the instinct to read the chapter before attempting
the problems has it backwards. Struggle first, briefly, then
read. The reading lands on a surface that's already been
prepared.
```

**D11 · Explaining**
```
The fastest way to find the holes in what you know is to try to
explain it to someone who doesn't.

Not summarize. Explain — in plain words, without the jargon,
answering the follow-up questions.

Jargon is where the gaps hide. As long as you can say
"contribution margin," you never have to demonstrate that you
know what it is. Take the phrase away and you either understand
it or you stall.

Every time I've stalled explaining something to a cook, the
stall was the real state of my knowledge and the fluency was
the illusion.
```

**D12 · Transfer**
```
The hardest thing in learning is not understanding an idea. It's
recognizing it somewhere it isn't labeled.

In a chapter on sunk cost, everyone gets sunk cost right. Two
weeks later, standing in front of an over-ordered walk-in,
almost nobody does.

The chapter provides the cue. Life doesn't.

Which is the argument for learning ideas attached to as many
different situations as possible. Not because the situations
matter, but because each one adds another route back to the idea
when there's no heading on the page.
```

**D13 · Practice vs experience**
```
Fifteen years of experience and one year repeated fifteen times
look identical on a resume.

The difference is whether the work included attempts at things
just past your current ability, with feedback fast enough to
correct.

Most jobs stop supplying that around year two. You reach
competent, the work stops being hard, and the clock keeps
running.

Time is not the input. Attempted difficulty with feedback is.

Which is why the person who's been there four years is sometimes
better than the person who's been there twelve, and why that
isn't unfair.
```

**D14 · Chunking**
```
An expert doesn't think faster. They think in bigger pieces.

A new cook sees eleven separate things on a ticket. Someone
experienced sees two patterns. Same information, same processing
speed, radically different load.

This is why expertise looks like calm rather than speed, and why
it can't be transferred by explanation. The chunks are built by
repetition, not description.

It's also why the expert is a bad judge of how hard the task is.
They can't see the eleven pieces anymore.
```

**D15 · Worked examples**
```
For a genuine beginner, being handed a problem to struggle with
is worse than being shown a finished solution.

Early on, working memory is fully consumed just holding the
pieces. There's nothing left to notice the pattern with.

A worked example — the whole thing, done, with the reasoning
visible — teaches faster. Then you fade the support: half done,
then the last step only, then nothing.

Struggle is productive after a foundation exists. Before that
it's just load.

Most on-the-job training gets the order exactly backwards.
```

**D16 · Testing as training**
```
The quiz isn't there to measure the training. The quiz is part of
the training.

Retrieval is what makes memory durable, so the act of being
tested does more for retention than the same time spent
reviewing.

This changes how you'd build a training program. Not: teach,
teach, teach, assess. Instead: small retrieval attempts
throughout, spaced out, low stakes, with correction.

The assessment at the end is for the record. The ones along the
way are for the learning, and they're the part most programs
skip.
```

**D17 · Forgetting curve**
```
You will lose most of what you learned today within a week, and
the schedule of that loss is predictable enough to plan around.

The drop is steep at first and then flattens. Which means the
timing of review matters far more than the amount.

A five-minute review at day one, day three, and day seven beats
an hour at day thirty, easily.

Practical version: whatever you learned today, look at it again
tomorrow for two minutes. That single habit does more than any
study technique I've tried.
```

**D18 · Notes**
```
Notes taken to capture everything are worse than notes taken to
capture the argument.

Verbatim transcription requires no processing, which is exactly
why it produces no learning. You can write down a sentence you
did not understand.

Notes in your own words force a decision about what the thing
means, and that decision is the learning event.

The test of a good note is whether it's useful in six months
without the source. Most notes fail that immediately, which is
why nobody rereads them.
```

**D19 · Base rates**
```
Before you ask how good this specific opportunity looks, ask how
often things in this category work at all.

Vivid, specific information overwhelms boring statistical
information almost every time. A compelling story about one
restaurant that made it will beat the closure rate for the whole
category, in your head, without your permission.

The base rate isn't the answer. It's the starting point that the
specifics should move you away from.

Most people skip straight to the specifics and never set the
starting point at all.
```

**D20 · Survivorship**
```
Every business book you've read is written about the companies
that survived, which is why they mostly don't work.

The traits shared by successful firms are only meaningful if you
also check whether the failed ones had them too. Almost no one
checks, because the failures don't write books and don't get
studied.

Bold vision, relentless focus, taking the big swing — the
companies that died had those in quantity.

You're reading a sample selected on the outcome. Any pattern you
find in it is partly the selection, and there's no way to tell
how much.
```

**D21 · Goodhart's law**
```
Every metric you attach a consequence to gets gamed, and usually
by good people.

Measure ticket times and food starts leaving the window before
it's ready. Measure food cost and quality quietly drops.
Measure covers and nobody wants the four-top that lingers.

Not corruption. The measure became the goal, and it was always a
partial proxy for the goal.

You can't solve this by finding a better measure. You solve it
by using several, changing them occasionally, and keeping
judgment in the loop.

Anyone promising you the one right number is selling something.
```

**D22 · Second-order**
```
"And then what happens?" is the highest-return question in
business and it gets asked far too rarely.

First-order thinking stops at the immediate result. Cut prices,
get more customers.

Second-order asks what follows. Competitor matches, margin
gone, volume up, kitchen strained, quality drops, the regulars
who came for quality leave, and you're now competing on price
with a worse product.

Everyone can do first-order. It's fast and it's usually right
about the immediate effect.

The advantage is entirely in the second and third steps, and
they only cost you the discipline to keep asking.
```

**D23 · Inversion**
```
Instead of asking how to make this work, ask what would
guarantee it fails. Then don't do those.

It's a genuinely different question and the answers arrive
faster.

How do I build a great training program is vague. What would
make a training program fail is specific and immediate: nobody
has time for it, the content is generic, there's no record of
completion, and the managers don't use it themselves.

Avoiding the known failures gets you most of the way there, and
the failures are much easier to enumerate than the successes.
```

**D24 · Falsifiability**
```
Ask yourself what would change your mind. If nothing would, you
don't have a belief, you have a position.

This is the cheapest intellectual habit available and almost
nobody runs it.

I believe restaurant operators will pay for training software.
What would change my mind: forty real conversations where the
problem is acknowledged and nobody will pay anything for it.

Now I have a test, a number, and a way to be wrong on purpose
rather than by accident.

Unfalsifiable confidence is how people spend four years finding
out.
```

**D25 · Models**
```
Every framework you learn is wrong. The useful question is what
it's wrong about and whether that matters here.

Five Forces ignores complements and moves slowly. Supply and
demand assumes information nobody has. Maslow's hierarchy
doesn't hold up as a strict sequence.

All still useful. A model is a deliberate simplification, and
the simplification is the feature — it's what lets you think at
all.

The failure isn't using an imperfect model. It's forgetting you
did, and mistaking the map for the thing.
```

**D26 · Reading**
```
Reading a textbook front to back is the least efficient way to
use one and it's the only way most people are taught.

Better sequence: read the chapter summary first. Then the
learning objectives. Then skim the headings to build the shape.
Then read, looking for answers to questions you now have.

You're not trying to absorb the chapter. You're trying to fill in
a structure you built before you started.

Same pages, a third of the time, and considerably more of it
retained — because you read it looking for something.
```

**D27 · Synthesis**
```
At the end of anything you learn, write one page from memory with
the source closed.

Not a summary of the material. The version you'd give someone
who has to make a decision tomorrow.

It forces three things at once: retrieval, selection of what
actually matters, and translation into plain language. Those are
the three operations that convert reading into knowledge.

It takes twenty minutes and it's the only artifact from a course
I've ever gone back to.

Everything else — highlights, slides, my own notes — I have never
opened again. Not once.
```
